Does a coarser decomposition -- fewer, larger boxes -- cut the iteration count?
The hypothesis was that the count tracks the number of interfaces a wave crosses,
q, so that halving q would roughly halve it.  Converging lens, omega/2pi = 256,
n = 2047, 16 threads, one process per row (examples/lsweep_compare.jl).

  julia --project=. -t 16 examples/lsweep_compare.jl 256 --only=lsweep8b96
  julia --project=. -t 16 examples/lsweep_compare.jl 256 --only=lsweep8b128
  julia --project=. -t 16 examples/lsweep_compare.jl 256 --only=lsweep16b128

  96          22    8     11.11s     1.513s    14    27.22s    18.9GB

VERDICT: refuted.  Halving q at fixed frequency and pad leaves the count where it
was: 14, 14, 15 at pad 8, and 10, 10 at pad 16.  The count is set by the pad in
wavelengths and by the frequency, not by how many interfaces there are.  Compare
128-point boxes at omega/2pi = 256 with 64-point boxes at 128: the same q = 16
and the same pad, yet 15 iterations against 7.  What doubled is the frequency --
the domain in wavelengths -- not the interface count.

So the account given earlier for the growth -- a per-interface reflection that
accumulates over q crossings -- is wrong, and has been withdrawn from the note.
What is left is the observation: at a pad fixed in wavelengths the count grows
about linearly with omega (7 -> 14 at one wavelength), and a thicker pad brings it
down (14, 10, 8 at one, two, three wavelengths).  Why the propagation distance in
wavelengths should matter at fixed per-interface absorption is open.

What larger boxes do buy is bytes.  The padded box carries (s + 2(2 + pad))^2
unknowns for s^2 owned, a redundancy of 1.72x at s = 64 and 1.34x at s = 128 with
pad 8, and 2.44x against 1.64x with pad 16.  Less redundancy means less factor
traffic per application and less memory: at pad 16 the 128-point boxes apply in
1.66s against 2.24s and peak at 21.3 GB against 27.0, for the same ten iterations.
128-point boxes with a two-wavelength pad is the fastest configuration measured at
this frequency, 14.4 + 22.0 = 36.4s to solution against 39.4s for the 64-point
one-wavelength default and 53.4s for the layered sweep.
